Written sources on Vajska are scarce online, so we are building this page step by step with the help of residents. This is what is confirmed so far.

## The name and the first record

There are two explanations of the village's name: from the nearby **Vajš** canal, or from **vejse**, fish traps and weirs that fish could swim into but not out of. Celts, Visigoths, Huns, Germanic tribes, Slavs, Avars and Tatars all passed through this corner of south-western Bačka, and according to Dnevnik the Hungarians settled here in the 9th century. Vajska is first recorded in **1338**, when five families lived in it ([Dnevnik](https://www.dnevnik.rs/vojvodina/foto-dnevnik-u-selu-kraj-vode-vajska-prirodno-jezero-provala-na-ponos-svih-mestana-22-04)).

## Before and after the First World War

Before the First World War the village was Catholic, with Hungarians, Germans and Croats. After the war 176 Orthodox families of **Salonika front volunteers** settled here ([Dnevnik](https://www.dnevnik.rs/vojvodina/foto-dnevnik-u-selu-kraj-vode-vajska-prirodno-jezero-provala-na-ponos-svih-mestana-22-04)); more about them on [The Salonika volunteers of Vajska]({{< relref "village/solunci" >}}). Today some twenty nationalities live in the village ([Life in Vajska]({{< relref "village/life" >}})).

## Old names

In historical lists of settlements the village also appears as **Tothwayzka**. The name belongs to a group of Danube villages with *Tót* in their names, where the Bačka **Šokci** lived or still live ([ZKVH, Naselje Šokac](https://zkvh.org.rs/storage/app/uploads/public/62f/f33/037/62ff33037caf8000026131.pdf)). Today's official names are Vajska, Hungarian *Vajszka* and *Józsefháza*, German *Wajska* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajska)). Their traditions are kept today in the [Šokac house]({{< relref "village/sokacka-kuca" >}}) in Vajska.

## A shrinking village

Between 1961 and 2011 the population fell from 4,355 to 2,834, by a third ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajska)). As in many villages of Bačka, young people move to the cities.

## The ferry to Borovo, 1991

After the fighting at Borovo Selo in Croatia, a Danube ferry between Vajska and Borovo started on 4 May 1991. It reportedly carried between 10,000 and 30,000 passengers, among them Croat refugees from Borovo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajska)).
